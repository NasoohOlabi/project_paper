\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}
\raggedbottom

The evaluation follows the message from a plausible comment to a recovered payload. We first ask whether the generated comments fit their conversations compared with ZLG. We then examine whether their reply and topic choices let a receiver recover a complete message. Finally, the archive experiments examine reception when the parties independently acquire their public context. A comment can read naturally yet carry a topic the receiver misidentifies, so each step answers a question that the preceding measurements alone cannot settle.

This progression connects the research questions to the evidence. The methodology explains the embedding mechanism and shared inputs; the quality comparisons assess the resulting text, while the recovery experiments examine generation, extraction, and their failure causes. Preparation and transport checks help distinguish those causes within the larger communication process.

We compared our comments with the official implementation of the ZLG baseline~\cite{lin2024zero} on two separate runs.
Table~\ref{tab:llm-judge} is a quality check. A language model scored a 244-post subset of the fixed comment-quality dataset, chosen where ZLG's~\cite{lin2024zero} output was unique.
Table~\ref{tab:paired-results} is the initial configuration comparison on capacity, GPT-2 perplexity, length, lexical quality, and BERTScore.
The two tables do not share a sample and do not measure the same thing.

Two setup facts apply to the two baseline comparisons.
\label{par:baseline-receiver-inputs}
First, our payloads were rebuilt from sender audit metadata, and the decoder could take the recorded topic index instead of identifying it from the comment. ZLG's~\cite{lin2024zero} reveal received the sender's token IDs and an externally supplied payload length. Supplying the IDs avoids reconstructing the token sequence from visible text; the length identifies where the payload ends. Each method therefore received different assistance. These comparisons do not determine which is more reliable when the receiver has only public text.
Second, the generation experiment behind the comment-quality comparison set \texttt{max\_retries} to 1, rather than the balanced-profile budget of 6 retries after the first attempt, so its success rate describes a lower-retry configuration.


Data hashes detect differences between the parties' inputs but do not ensure stable web results or matching copies of the post's comments. These comparisons do not test a receiver rebuilding the search and topic lists from the public conversation alone.

\subsection{Baseline selection and comparison conditions}
\label{sec:baseline-choice}

Assessing the proposed improvements required a reference method, and finding a directly comparable method was difficult. Our method carries bits through reply-location and comment-topic choices, calls a language model through an API, and uses domain texts to construct topics. The reviewed methods differ in their information-carrying choices, shared data, and model-access requirements. These differences affect capacity, text quality, and extraction conditions, so comparing scores published on different datasets cannot establish improvement. Components also have precedents: LLM-Stega~\cite{wu2024generative} already uses black-box prompting and text verification, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:related_work}.

We selected ZLG~\cite{lin2024zero} because it generates carrier text without task-specific training and has an official implementation\footnote{\url{https://github.com/leonardodalinky/zero-shot-GLS}} that we could run on the same source posts. This permits a common-context comparison instead of relying on results from unrelated datasets. The comparison addresses specific comment-quality criteria and the recorded outcomes under the tested settings; different criteria can favor different methods.

\paragraph{Model access and choice.}
\label{par:baseline-model-access}
ZLG~\cite{lin2024zero} needs token probabilities to carry bits in generation choices. Our method calls the model through an API to write comments and identify their topics. Its design can therefore use a compatible model that does not expose token probabilities, including one offered by an external service. Evaluating both methods with a locally runnable model covers only part of the model choices available to our design.

\label{par:baseline-model-scale}
To reduce the effect of differing model capability, the comment-quality experiment used local models from the Qwen 3.5 9B family for both methods. Sharing a model family does not imply identical quantization, runtime, or retry policies. This comparison did not evaluate our method with a larger model or one with stronger reasoning capabilities. A wider context window could accommodate more source text, and a stronger model might extract more varied topics and identify them more reliably. These remain potential benefits requiring a controlled comparison.

The value of more topics depends on whether their choices remain distinguishable, as explained in Section~\ref{par:topic-count-choice}. A stronger model's effect on that tradeoff requires a controlled comparison.

\paragraph{How the channels carry larger messages.}
\label{par:baseline-capacity}
ZLG~\cite{lin2024zero} can carry additional bits through additional generated tokens, within generation and acceptance limits. In our method, a longer comment alone does not enlarge the budget of its reply-location and topic choices. A larger message needs further choices carried by other comments, using the frames in Section~\ref{sec:multiframe}. Payload growth can thus mean a longer text for the baseline or more comments for our method. Comparing larger messages requires accounting for carrier count, total words, recovered bits, and failures together.

The initial comparisons used the single-comment path. Their \texttt{capacity\_matched} setting attempted to approximate each sample's budget for ZLG by rounding to bytes and selecting a payload excerpt; it did not establish identical recoverable useful payloads. The reported 16.00 bits in the initial comparison is neither a fixed target imposed on every sample nor ZLG's maximum capacity. This setting limits what the comparison reveals about capacity growth in both methods and does not evaluate our multi-comment path.

\subsection{How the printed metrics are computed}

The available fixed-width selection budget for two independently coded power-of-two subsets is $3+5=8$ bits when there are 8 reply targets and 32 candidate topics. In general this safe budget is
\[
\lfloor\log_2 C\rfloor + \lfloor\log_2 A\rfloor,
\]
where $C$ is the number of reply targets (flattened comments plus the post) and $A$ is the number of candidate comment topics.
For non-power-of-two choice counts, leftover physical index states are excluded; for example, $C=3,A=5$ yield $1+2=3$ distinguishable bits, while modulo aliases the remaining states. This is a fixed-width selection budget, not Shannon capacity or achieved useful throughput.
In the comment-quality dataset, the stored 13.42 bits/comment for our method is this available budget, not a measurement of independently embedded or recovered useful payload. The audit-assisted reconstruction does not establish receiver recovery. The ZLG~\cite{lin2024zero} values describe its reported successful payload in a capacity-matched setting, not its native token-channel maximum, and its reveal used sender token IDs plus externally supplied payload length.

Success rates count every attempted sample, including failures.
Quality scores, including the judge table, are computed only on accepted generations.
Failed attempts stay in the attempt counts.

Several table cells average inside each source post first, then across posts.
That makes the post the independent unit when several comments share a thread.

GPT-2 perplexity is how surprised a GPT-2 model is by the comment.
Per text it is $\exp(\sum \mathrm{NLL}/\sum \text{scored tokens})$, then averaged.
Lower means more GPT-2-predictable, not more human.
The fixed comment-quality dataset did not score GPT-2, unigram KL, JSD, BLEU, ROUGE, or BERTScore.
Those columns appear only in Table~\ref{tab:paired-results}.

The lexical-quality index is a 0--100 score from the comparison builder, version \texttt{lexical\_quality\_v2}.
It is $100\times(0.55\,\mathrm{MATTR}+0.30\,\text{bigram score}+0.15\,\text{length sanity})$.
MATTR is a moving-average type-token ratio with window 10.
The bigram term penalizes a repeated two-word phrase.
Length sanity is 1 for comments between 5 and 120 tokens and falls off outside that band.
It is not a raw MATTR column.

Word count in both printed tables is the number of \texttt{[A-Za-z0-9']+} tokens.
The later publication runner uses a different word-boundary regex.
Those lengths are not in these tables.

BERTScore F1 in Table~\ref{tab:paired-results} is the unrescaled figure from the initial configuration report.
It is overlap with a human reference comment, not a security score.

The LLM judge is not G-Eval.
G-Eval is a separate 1--5 quality path with a matched human reference.
Here we asked Codex Luna, at high reasoning, five blinded questions about already-generated comments.
Lineups are seeded.
The model is not deterministic.
These scores are not a reader study, and they are not proof of authorship or security.

We took one our-method comment and one ZLG~\cite{lin2024zero} comment per post.
The source freeze had 1{,}608 complete pairs from 339 posts.
The judge set keeps, in sorted pair order, the first pair per post whose ZLG~\cite{lin2024zero} comment is unique in that pool.
That yields 244 pairs from 244 posts, 244 unique texts on each arm.
Ninety-five posts with no unique ZLG~\cite{lin2024zero} output were dropped, so repeated ZLG~\cite{lin2024zero} covers would not dominate the scores.
The judge set is therefore a unique-ZLG subset~\cite{lin2024zero}, not a random sample of the 339 posts.
The five checks reuse those pairs.
They are not 2{,}928 independent generated samples.
All 2{,}928 judge tasks completed with no task errors.

Figure~\ref{fig:judge-standout} and the additional figures in Appendix~\ref{app:judge-material} illustrate all five checks using the same post: \texttt{1lqu69t} on r/news, ``First immigration detainees arrive at Florida center in the Everglades.'' Chance is 10\%.

\subsubsection{Weakest Text}
One lineup holds a human comment, our comment, and the ZLG~\cite{lin2024zero} comment.
The judge names the weakest of the three.
Chance is 33\%.
Lower is better.
The three pick rates share the same 244 lineups and sum to 100\%.
Figure~\ref{fig:judge-weak-link} describes this test on the worked example: the judge ranked ZLG's~\cite{lin2024zero} terse reply as most human-plausible, the human control's comment second, and named our comment weakest, calling it ``vague, polished abstraction [that] does not clearly engage the thread.''

\subsubsection{Suspicion AUROC}
Each arm, including a held-out human comment, gets a 0--100 suspicion score.
AUROC here is how often that method's comments look more suspicious than the human comments.
Ties count as 0.5.
Chance is 0.5.
Lower is closer to the human scores.
The reported $p=0.385$ tests the paired raw scores, not the AUROC gap.
Figure~\ref{fig:judge-suspicion} shows this test on the worked example, where the scores are unusually clean: our comment scored 82/100, flagged for four tells including \texttt{generic\_could\_fit\_any\_thread} and \texttt{register\_mismatch}; ZLG~\cite{lin2024zero} scored 10/100, ``no clear machine-generated signals''; the human control scored 3/100.
The example's wide score gap does not hold up across all 244 pairs: AUROC is 0.661 for our method versus 0.650 for ZLG~\cite{lin2024zero}, and a paired test of the raw suspicion scores does not show a clear difference ($p=0.385$).
Each text is scored alone, with no lineup and no knowledge of the other arms.
The full prompt is reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:judge-material}.



\subsubsection{Thread Attribution}
The judge picks which of four thread blurbs a comment belongs to.
Chance is 25\%.
Higher means stronger thread-specific linkage.
We report the rate.
We did not run a significance test on this row.

\subsection{How often generation succeeded}

These counts are not the 244-pair judge set.

The run that fed the comparison requested 4{,}044 samples and recorded 2{,}543 successes and 1{,}501 failures (846 generation failures, 613 comment topic mismatches, 42 invalid JSON).
Every recorded success reconstructed the compressed bitstring from sender-side audit metadata.
That is an engineering check, not Bob reading a public thread alone.
\label{par:baseline-retries}
The campaign allowed one retry, whereas the balanced implementation profile permits six after the first attempt. This gives the sender fewer opportunities to obtain an acceptable comment whose topic is correctly identified. It is a resource setting for this experiment; the method allows other retry limits. \subsection{Validating list reconstruction during development}
\label{sec:reconstruction-validation}

The quality comparisons assess the generated text. To examine extraction, we distinguish two possible sources of disagreement: the parties may build different lists, or they may identify different topics within matching lists. Repeated preparation checks the first; the message experiments examine the second alongside complete recovery.

During development, we test whether the parties can build the same lists by processing the same post independently twice and comparing the outputs. This is a research validation procedure, not an additional step the sender performs for every message. In use, the sender processes the post to prepare the message, and the receiver reconstructs the lists during extraction, using the agreed inputs and settings.

The reconstruction test covers search-query generation, document-text collection, and comment-topic extraction. Each pass performs these stages independently. Corresponding stage outputs are then compared to check agreement in the texts, lists, and ordering. This procedure does not require processing every dataset post twice: development validation of reconstruction is separate from preparation of the experimental dataset.

Successful message transmission requires a separate test. After the sender selects the reply location and topic and generates the comment, we attempt to extract the message from that comment. We therefore distinguish agreement in the parties' lists from the receiver's ability to identify the choices and extract the bits. The following sections specify the receiver inputs and the scope of each experiment.

\subsection{Exploratory reply-chain alignment probe}
\label{sec:reply-chain-probe}

A diagnostic probe on the frozen post \texttt{1vygsir} found that the post-wide topic list could pair a reply to a college comment with a topic quoted from a sibling discussion about a restaurant.
The writer followed the selected parent and produced college-related text, while the receiver could not identify the unrelated restaurant topic.
This was a source-alignment failure, not an angle-index mapping error.

We then enabled the exploratory reply-chain mode, which rebuilds the topic list from the post and the selected parent chain after selecting the reply target.
Because this post has no body, the title supplied the post-level input; the resulting codebook contained two topics and excluded sibling comments.
A 64-bit payload required 19 frames with 20 available.
GPT-6 Luna generated a first-frame reply that passed strict decoding against both codebook entries supplied to an in-memory semantic adapter.
In the first 25 frozen posts, 13 could be planned within 20 frames in the extractive setting.

This is an exploratory capacity and first-frame check, not a full-payload recovery result.
The semantic adapter bypassed the normal semantic-search service, and the remaining frames were not generated or decoded.
These figures are separate from the initial comparisons and do not establish an end-to-end recovery rate.

\subsection{Source-excerpt recovery experiment}
\label{sec:october-recovery}
The next question is whether readable choices can carry a complete message across several comments. This experiment holds the prepared context shared, allowing us to examine generation, topic identification, and message assembly together. Source excerpts provide the initial payload condition; the independent-payload check below then removes that source-text selection.

The source-excerpt recovery experiment contains \OctAttempts{} source-excerpt payloads generated with Qwen 9B under the contract in Table~\ref{tab:recovery-contract}. The excerpts favor dictionary matches and are not independently assigned arbitrary messages. Of all assigned messages, \OctExact/\OctAttempts{} recovered exactly; \OctGenerationFailures{} failed generation and \OctReceiverFailures{} failed receiver recovery. Among complete generated sequences, recovery was \OctExact/\OctComplete{}. The all-attempt proportion is 28\%, with a 95\% Wilson interval of [\OctWilsonLow\%, \OctWilsonHigh\%]. This interval describes a convenience batch and does not establish population-wide reliability.

A replay of saved classifier outputs through the real receiver reproduced all \OctComplete{} complete-sequence outcomes without new LLM calls. Among \OctFrames{} carriers, \OctCorrectFrames{} had the correct topic selection and \OctConfusedFrames{} had an incorrect selection despite the intended topic being in the receiver shortlist. Every one of the \OctReceiverFailures{} receiver-failed messages included such a confusion. In \OctIdenticalPromptConfusions{} confused frames, recorded sender and receiver prompts were identical; two others lacked a complete prompt record. Codebook and dictionary comparisons found no identity disagreement in the complete traces. These observations locate the saved failures at classification; they do not prove that a particular sampling change repairs them. No silent payload mismatch was observed, which is not a bound on its future probability.

All \OctAttempts{} offline codec/transport checks passed. This differs from live recovery. Paired planning on the same source excerpts reduced compressed streams from 844 to 779 bits (7.70\%), framed streams from 2,522 to 2,419 bits (4.08\%), and planned replies from 198 to 187 (5.56\%). The \OctFrames{} saved carriers cover complete sequences; six further replies appear in aborted private traces and belong in generation-cost accounting. There is no matched baseline or naturalness assessment for this batch.

\subsubsection{Bounded Luna development check}
The separate independent-payload development experiment assigned independent eight-byte printable payloads (64 UTF-8 storage bits) to ten development posts under the same shared-state contract. Planning covered 32, 64 and 128 bits at limits of 8, 12 and 20 carriers; all ten fit the primary 64-bit/20-carrier cell. Generation used one retry and fresh Codex CLI calls requesting GPT-6 Luna, followed by an independent receiver using ordinary MiniLM retrieval. CLI temperature control was unavailable, so this is neither a test of the classifier-temperature correction nor a matched model comparison.

The prespecified engineering gate required 8/10 exact messages. Collection stopped at its third failure: \DevExact{} of \DevAttempted{} attempted messages recovered exactly, and \DevUnattempted{} remained unattempted. Every attempted message generated completely; \DevWrong{} of \DevFrames{} receiver selections were wrong despite target inclusion. Those four fresh responses differed from recorded sender responses to identical prompts. Two messages failed CRC; the third could not reconstruct a valid framed stream. No silent mismatch was observed. The unattempted cases were the title-only posts, so their live feasibility remains open. This stopped development outcome is not a fixed-size reliability estimate and cannot justify expansion to confirmation.

Fresh search and codebook extraction costs are excluded because prepared contexts were reused. \subsection{What these comparisons do not show}

\paragraph{Domain texts and word-distribution metrics.}
\label{par:baseline-domain-vocabulary}
Domain texts broaden the topics related to the post in our method. They may introduce vocabulary uncommon in the human reference comments, changing the unigram distributions measured by KL and JSD. Our ZLG~\cite{lin2024zero} adaptation receives examples from the post context but does not use the same domain-document research stage. Divergence can therefore reflect content differences as well as writing style, and is also affected by text length and vocabulary sparsity. This is a possible explanation: the experiments did not isolate domain research through an otherwise matched comparison with research enabled and disabled. These metrics were not computed on the comment-quality dataset and cannot be inferred from the judge scores.

The judge result is about the unique-ZLG-output subset~\cite{lin2024zero}, one pair per post.
The initial configuration table covers one comment-topic capacity configuration and one baseline service configuration, with reused posts and audit-assisted reconstruction on our side.
KL/JSD, synthetic detection, and grouped passive-detector ROC-AUC are computed by later runners and are not in the fixed comment-quality dataset.
A dedicated steganalysis and editing-attack evaluation remains future work.
This paper does not claim a security advantage for either method.
